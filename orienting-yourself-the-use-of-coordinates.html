Chapter 1: Orienting Yourself — The Use of Coordinates

Class 9 Mathematics · Worked Examples · Questions and Solutions

Chapter Overview

This chapter introduces the Cartesian coordinate plane, ordered pairs, axes, quadrants and the use of coordinates to locate points.

Point = (x-coordinate, y-coordinate)
  • The x-axis is horizontal.
  • The y-axis is vertical.
  • The origin is (0, 0).
  • Points on the axes do not belong to any quadrant.

Q1. Coordinates of the intersection of the axes

Find the coordinates of the point where the x-axis and y-axis intersect.

Show Solution
The x-axis and y-axis intersect at the origin. At this point, both coordinates are zero.
(0, 0)
Answer: O = (0, 0).

Q2. A point on a vertical line with x = −5

Describe the possible positions of a point H whose x-coordinate is −5.

Show Solution
Every point on this vertical line has x-coordinate −5. Therefore, its general coordinates are H = (−5, y), where y may be any real number.
  • If y is positive, H lies in Quadrant II.
  • If y is negative, H lies in Quadrant III.
  • If y = 0, H lies on the negative x-axis.
Answer: The point can lie in Quadrant II, Quadrant III, or on the negative x-axis.

Q3. Properties of quadrilateral RAMP

Given R = (3, 0), A = (0, −2), M = (−5, −2) and P = (−5, 2), identify perpendicular sides, sides parallel to the axes and points that are reflections in the x-axis.

Show Solution
Perpendicular sides: AM is horizontal and MP is vertical. Hence, AM ⟂ MP.

Parallel to the x-axis: A and M have the same y-coordinate, −2. Therefore, AM is parallel to the x-axis.

Parallel to the y-axis: M and P have the same x-coordinate, −5. Therefore, MP is parallel to the y-axis.

Reflections: M = (−5, −2) and P = (−5, 2) have the same x-coordinate and opposite y-coordinates. They are reflections of each other in the x-axis.

Q4. Right-angled triangle using Z = (5, −6)

Construct a right-angled triangle using the coordinate axes and Z = (5, −6). Find its side lengths.

Show Solution
Choose I = (5, 0) and N = (0, −6). The points O = (0, 0), I and N form a right-angled triangle with Z as the opposite corner of the rectangle OIZN.

Vertical length:
IZ = |0 − (−6)| = 6 units
Horizontal length:
ZN = |5 − 0| = 5 units
Using Pythagoras:
IN² = 6² + 5² = 61
IN = √61 units ≈ 7.81 units
Answer: The perpendicular side lengths are 5 and 6 units, and the hypotenuse is √61 units.

Q5. What if negative numbers did not exist?

Could we locate every point on the coordinate plane if negative numbers did not exist? Explain.

Show Solution
No. Without negative numbers, coordinates could only be zero or positive. We could locate points in Quadrant I and on the positive portions of the axes, but not points to the left of the y-axis or below the x-axis.

Answer: Negative numbers are necessary to represent points in Quadrants II, III and IV.

Q6. Check whether M, A and G are collinear

Given M = (−3, −4), A = (0, 0) and G = (6, 8), determine whether the three points lie on the same straight line.

Show Solution
Slope of MA:
m = [0 − (−4)] / [0 − (−3)] = 4/3
Slope of AG:
m = (8 − 0) / (6 − 0) = 8/6 = 4/3
Both slopes are equal.
m(MA) = m(AG) = 4/3
Answer: M, A and G are collinear.

Q7. Check whether R, B and C are collinear

Given R = (−5, −1), B = (−2, −5) and C = (4, −12), determine whether the points are collinear.

Show Solution
Slope of RB:
m(RB) = [−5 − (−1)] / [−2 − (−5)] = −4/3
Slope of BC:
m(BC) = [−12 − (−5)] / [4 − (−2)] = −7/6
Since −4/3 is not equal to −7/6, the slopes are different.

Answer: R, B and C are not collinear.

Q8. Construct triangles with the origin as a vertex

(i) Give an example of a right-angled isosceles triangle with the origin as a vertex.

(ii) Give an example of an isosceles triangle whose other two vertices lie in Quadrants III and IV.

Show Solution
(i) Right-angled isosceles triangle
Choose O = (0, 0), A = (4, 0) and B = (0, 4).
OA = 4, OB = 4, and OA ⟂ OB
The two perpendicular sides are equal, so the triangle is right-angled and isosceles.

(ii) Isosceles triangle
Choose O = (0, 0), P = (−3, −4) and Q = (3, −4).
OP = √(9 + 16) = 5
OQ = √(9 + 16) = 5
Since OP = OQ, the triangle is isosceles. P lies in Quadrant III and Q lies in Quadrant IV.

Q9. Determine whether M is the midpoint of ST

Check each set of coordinates using the condition SM = MT.

Show Solution
Set 1: S = (−3, 0), M = (0, 0), T = (3, 0).
SM = 3 and MT = 3. Yes, M is the midpoint.

Set 2: S = (2, 3), M = (3, 4), T = (4, 5).
SM = √2 and MT = √2. Yes, M is the midpoint.

Set 3: S = (0, 0), M = (0, 5), T = (0, −10).
SM = 5 and MT = 15. No, M is not the midpoint.

Set 4: S = (−8, 7), M = (0, −2), T = (6, −3).
SM = √145 and MT = √37. No, M is not the midpoint.

Midpoint formula:
M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)

Q10. Find the missing endpoint B

The midpoint of AB is M = (−7, 1), and A = (3, −4). Find B = (x, y).

Show Solution
Using the midpoint formula:
((3 + x)/2, (−4 + y)/2) = (−7, 1)
Compare x-coordinates:
(3 + x)/2 = −7
3 + x = −14, so x = −17.

Compare y-coordinates:
(−4 + y)/2 = 1
−4 + y = 2, so y = 6.
B = (−17, 6)
Answer: B = (−17, 6).

Q11. Find the trisection points P and Q

The line segment AB has endpoints A = (4, 7) and B = (16, −2). Find P and Q such that AP = PQ = QB.

Show Solution
Total change from A to B:
(16 − 4, −2 − 7) = (12, −9)
One-third of this change is (4, −3).
P = (4 + 4, 7 − 3) = (8, 4)
Two-thirds of the change is (8, −6).
Q = (4 + 8, 7 − 6) = (12, 1)
Answer: P = (8, 4) and Q = (12, 1).

Q12. Points on and inside a circle

(i) Check whether A = (1, −8), B = (−4, 7) and C = (−7, −4) lie on a circle centred at the origin.

(ii) Determine whether D = (−5, 6) and E = (0, 9) lie inside or outside that circle.

Show Solution
Distance from the origin:
d = √(x² + y²)
For A:
OA = √(1² + (−8)²) = √65
For B:
OB = √((−4)² + 7²) = √65
For C:
OC = √((−7)² + (−4)²) = √65
All three distances are equal, so the circle has radius √65 units.

For D:
OD = √(25 + 36) = √61
Since √61 < √65, D lies inside the circle.

For E:
OE = √(0² + 9²) = 9
Since 9 > √65, E lies outside the circle.

Q13. Find the vertices of a triangle from its side midpoints

The midpoints of BC, CA and AB are D = (5, 1), E = (6, 5) and F = (0, 3), respectively. Find A, B and C.

Show Solution
Let A = (x₁, y₁), B = (x₂, y₂) and C = (x₃, y₃).

From D, the midpoint of BC:
x₂ + x₃ = 10; y₂ + y₃ = 2 (1)
From E, the midpoint of CA:
x₁ + x₃ = 12; y₁ + y₃ = 10 (2)
From F, the midpoint of AB:
x₁ + x₂ = 0; y₁ + y₂ = 6 (3)
Adding the x-coordinate equations (2) and (3), and using (1):
2x₁ + 10 = 12, so x₁ = 1
Then x₂ = −1 and x₃ = 11.

For the y-coordinates:
2y₁ + 2 = 16, so y₁ = 7
Then y₂ = −1 and y₃ = 3.
A = (1, 7), B = (−1, −1), C = (11, 3)

Q14. Model a city using coordinates and a scale

Represent streets that are 200 m apart using a coordinate grid. Use a scale of 1 cm = 200 m.

Show Solution
Draw the main north–south road as the y-axis and the main east–west road as the x-axis. Their intersection is the origin.

Using the scale 1 cm = 200 m, draw parallel streets at 1 cm intervals.

If the numbered streets are counted on both sides of the main road, there are two possible streets at each positive or negative distance.

For (4, 3), there are 2 choices for the fourth north–south street and 2 choices for the third east–west street:
2 × 2 = 4 intersections
For (3, 4), there are likewise:
2 × 2 = 4 intersections
Answer: Four intersections for each coordinate description, under this two-sided street interpretation.

Q15. Two circular icons on a computer screen

A screen is 800 pixels wide and 600 pixels high. Circle A has centre (100, 150) and radius 80 pixels. Circle B has centre (250, 230) and radius 100 pixels.

(i) Do the circles extend beyond the screen?

(ii) Do the circles intersect?

Show Solution
(i) Circle A:
x: 100 − 80 = 20 to 100 + 80 = 180
y: 150 − 80 = 70 to 150 + 80 = 230
These values lie within the screen boundaries.

Circle B:
x: 250 − 100 = 150 to 250 + 100 = 350
y: 230 − 100 = 130 to 230 + 100 = 330
These values also lie within the screen boundaries. Neither circle extends outside the screen.

(ii) Distance between centres:
AB = √((250 − 100)² + (230 − 150)²)
AB = √(150² + 80²) = √28900 = 170
The sum of the radii is 80 + 100 = 180, and their difference is 20.

Since 20 < 170 < 180, the circles intersect at two points.

Q16. Determine whether ABCD is a square and find its area

Given A = (2, 1), B = (−1, 2), C = (−2, −1) and D = (1, −2), determine whether ABCD is a square. Find its area.

Show Solution
Length AB:
AB = √((−1 − 2)² + (2 − 1)²) = √10
Similarly, BC = CD = DA = √10. Thus, all four sides are equal.

Slope of AB:
m₁ = (2 − 1)/(−1 − 2) = −1/3
Slope of BC:
m₂ = (−1 − 2)/(−2 − (−1)) = 3
Their product is:
m₁m₂ = (−1/3)(3) = −1
Therefore, AB and BC are perpendicular. The quadrilateral has equal sides and a right angle, so it is a square.

Area:
Area = side² = (√10)² = 10 square units
Answer: ABCD is a square of area 10 square units.

Quick Revision

  • Origin: (0, 0).
  • Quadrant I: (+, +).
  • Quadrant II: (−, +).
  • Quadrant III: (−, −).
  • Quadrant IV: (+, −).
  • Distance formula: d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²).
  • Midpoint formula: ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2).
  • Collinear points have equal slopes between consecutive points, where slopes are defined.
